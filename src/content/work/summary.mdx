## Context

In March 2026, two months into my full-time NAPP role, an editor I'd been working with on Search asked if I could help with something different. He was leading a summary workstream inside the [Newsroom AI Initiative team](https://www.nytco.com/press/introducing-the-a-i-initiatives-team/) — a separate group from NAPP, sitting inside the newsroom and focused on AI tools for reporters and editors. They wanted help thinking about how AI-generated summaries should appear to readers.

## Approach

Most AI summaries on the market are text-based and quick, optimized to save the reader time. NYT's bet runs the other way: summary as an *additive* layer, visual and interactive, designed to pull readers *into* the reporting rather than past it.

## Exploration

The quickest way to figure out what "additive, visual, interactive" actually meant for the Times was to sketch. The internal tool produced text; the design question was how that text should appear to readers. I lined up three directions: an inline one-sentence summary, an inline bullet breakdown, and a full-screen story-card I sketched as the alternative.

<Figure
  wide
  src="/media/summary/internal-tool-styles.png"
  alt="The same NYT article shown three ways: an inline A.I. Summary panel, an inline Key Takeaways bullet panel, and a full-screen Article Overview story-card"
  caption="Three reader-facing summary directions on a single article: a one-sentence summary, a bullet breakdown, a full-screen story-card."
/>

The story-card was the one that resonated. Building out the fuller experience meant working with an editor to shape a five-slide narrative arc. The card had to carry the tangible and informational pieces (a number, a quote, an image) that anchor a story, with a final slide that returns the reader to the reporting.

Once a static design feels decent, I'd like to move into prototyping as soon as possible, so the experience feels real instead of imagined. A static frame couldn't answer the questions tap-forward poses about rhythm, entry, and the return to the reporting.

The other constraint was NYT visual identity. The card borrows a familiar tap-forward pattern from Instagram, Snap, TikTok; the small signals that make a frame recognizably NYT (masthead, typography, photo treatment, byline) had to be designed in, so a shared frame still reads as NYT, not generic content.

Article summary is one application of the same primitives. The same idea could power a "catch me up" surface, a home-tab digest, a multi-article briefing, a multi-source synthesis. The expansion wasn't only mine; leadership conversations and work-shares I sat in on were already pointing at the same shape. I sketched what summary as a *capability* could look like across NYT, using The Athletic as the working example.

<Figure
  wide
  src="/media/summary/summary-as-capability.png"
  alt="Summary as a capability, sketched on The Athletic: the same primitives feeding multiple entry points (article tap, home-tab modules, lock-screen notifications), each producing a tap-forward sequence shaped to the content"
  caption="Summary as a capability, sketched on The Athletic: one set of primitives, multiple entry points (article tap, home-tab modules, lock-screen notifications), each producing a tap-forward sequence."
/>

The shape I've been sketching uses Scoop, NYT's CMS, as the foundation. An editor in Scoop produces up to three base summary types per article: a paragraph, a bullet or multi-slide breakdown, and a richer one organized around evidence. Each surface picks the base it needs and applies its own prompt at request time. The implication for editorial work is real: editors review AI-generated variants and approve what goes out, alongside the older work of writing and shaping the story.

## Outcome

Design makes the abstract explicit. The summary work has been discussed more and more across teams: people share prototype links, the work has come up in meetings, conversations have sparked from it. The Athletic product team reached out to discuss the project further. I'm hoping they partner with NAPP to build it for H2.

## Reflection

The thing I'm taking forward from this work is a sharper sense of what design is for. A system diagram describes a possible future; a card you can swipe through *is* that future, in miniature, a thing the room can hold and react to. The diagram needs the card to mean anything to anyone. The magic, when it works, is small: making something abstract feel real enough to argue with.

I want to keep doing this, and I want more room for design to show up earlier, as a strategic partner in conversations with business, masthead, and the people setting direction, not only as the team translating decisions later.

I'm also learning to be okay with not knowing the fate of the project. What I can invest in fully is the process: my craft, thinking, skill, passion. That's the only thing I really influence, and the thing that matters to me most.
