## Context

In 2024 I was lead product designer on the Ad Mission. I asked my manager if I could also work on Search, because NYT search was so bad that most readers found NYT articles through Google rather than through NYT itself. He let me try. Search became my passion project for the next two years.

ChatGPT had been out for a while, and reader behavior was shifting fast from keyword search to expecting synthesized answers. NYT's search was still lexical<Fn n={1} />: type a word, hope an article matches it. On the side of day jobs, we sketched what an alternative could look like (AI summaries, timelines, multi-modal answers, related questions, interactive formats), concrete enough to build conviction with newsroom partners and business leadership, and to articulate what a new architecture would need.

<Margin kind="footnote" n={1}>
  Lexical search ranks documents by literal word overlap. Semantic search matches by meaning using embeddings, a different retrieval architecture entirely.
</Margin>

In January 2026, NYT formed a new mission, NAPP (New AI Products and Platforms). I joined as the first and currently only full-time designer leading design for NYT Search.

## Approach

NYT search shouldn't try to be Google Gemini. It should be the answer only NYT can give: grounded in our reporting, with editorial standards baked into how summaries are written, how contested questions are presented, when to surface visual evidence instead of a flat yes/no. AI search can foreground that evidence rather than flatten it.

<Quote cite="from user research">
  NYT's AI answer felt like "show me the answer." Gemini felt like "tell me the answer."
</Quote>

### Intent-first

A central structural choice: NYT search is built around user intent, not keyword matching. Queries pass through an intent analyzer that parses possible intents and generates outputs matched to them. Working with engineers and PMs, I helped crystallize the six major intents<Fn n={2} /> we now design and build for. The long-term thesis is a multi-intent world. In the short term we ship one intent at a time, starting with **Latest News**.

<Margin kind="footnote" n={2}>
  The six intents: Latest News, Q&A, Navigational, Topic, Recommendation, Specific Retrieval.
</Margin>

## Exploration

The tool I reach for depends on how clear the idea is. When the system or the idea is fuzzy, I prototype to figure things out as I build. When I have a clear visual idea, I sketch it in Figma first. Locking the visual in before I build gets me to a better working prototype faster.

Two examples from this work.

**1. A fuzzy system.** Early on we didn't really know how the intent system would work. The team came together on a FigJam whiteboard and quickly sketched out the system as we thought through it. As a quick follow-up, we built it into a working interactive prototype, a place where we could test different search queries, play around, and see how the system actually behaved. The prototype is what gave us shared understanding.

### The last 10% push

With the structure and design of intent-mapped outputs in a good place, the work that mattered most was the final layer of polish, the moments where the gap between a thoughtful experience and a generic one is small but felt.

Two examples, one in motion and one in visual.

**Motion: pushed in code.** Loading states, the chain-of-thought reveal, the failure mode. These are timing problems, not layout problems. I built motion directly in code, with the final pixel pushing happening in real CSS against real content.

<Video
  wide
  src="/media/nyt-search/latest-news-interaction.mp4"
  alt="Working prototype: query → loading → chain-of-thought reveal → answer card."
  caption="Motion in code. Query → loading → chain-of-thought reveal → answer card."
/>

**Visual: sketched in Figma, checked in code.** Most of the visual refinement happened in Figma: sketching alternatives, pushing pixels, comparing variants. Code came in at the end as a sanity check against real content. The example I keep coming back to is how we marked AI-generated content inside the answer card. Earlier versions used a small *"A.I. introduction of Times reporting"* line with a BETA badge, readable but easy to miss. The label kept losing the visual hierarchy fight against the editorial copy itself. We refined to a clear *"Overview"* heading at the top of the card, with the AI disclosure as a sub-label directly below. Same intent, different trust signal.

<Margin kind="note">
  The first 90% (where the AI summary sits, what it contains, how citations work) felt like the heavy lift. The last 10%, what the label looks like, shifted the experience from feeling LLM-generic to feeling NYT.
</Margin>

## Outcome

The Latest News answer card is preparing for 2026 H1 beta launch, NYT's first generated-text format reaching real readers in product. It went through four rounds of internal editorial evaluation and one round of qualitative research. The bar isn't first-use clicks; it's whether readers come back. From here the work scales in two directions: more intents on the same pipeline (Q&A, Navigational, Topic, Recommendation, Specific Retrieval), and search-as-ask beyond the search page, wherever a reader has a question worth asking the Times.

## Reflection

Designing for AI features means understanding the underlying tech, including how the prompt works. I'm grateful for generous engineering partners who made it easy to ask about technical structure when I needed to. One example: the answer card had an early bridging line generated by the model that kept growing long and hedging. Once I understood the prompt fully, the fix was to take that work *off* the prompt and replace it with a static UI label, *"From Times coverage."* The prompt got simpler, the output cleaner, the reader got clarity.

<Figure
  wide
  src="/media/nyt-search/bridging-line-before-after.png"
  alt="Before/after of the bridging-line example — earlier verbose model-generated intro vs. the static 'From Times coverage' UI label that replaced it."
  caption="The fix: take the bridging job off the prompt and give it back to the UI."
/>

AI doesn't take the human work out of any of the roles around me; it sharpens what each one is for. Editors hold the line on judgment and voice. Engineers write prompts as a craft, not a quick task. PMs keep the vision sharp. Design pursues clarity for the reader, again and again. None of these is replaceable, and the collaboration is what makes the whole thing work.

What I'm most excited and curious about is where the NYT newsroom goes in embracing this transformative technology. More to figure out. More to build.
