## Context

When I joined Ad Mission in September 2021 as the first product designer, ad design didn't exist as a practice at NYT. Ads were treated as a default disruption, the cost of running the business, tolerated by readers, designed around reluctantly. The role existed because leadership wanted ad design to have its own seat: someone whose first question was *what's best for the reader experience even when an ad is on screen*, someone who could push toward premium, complementary advertising rather than the disruptive default.

The team's design practice scaled with the work. A second product designer joined, then a design manager to run day-to-day. I remained the most senior design IC throughout. Ad Mission shifted from implementing ad ideas handed to it by other teams, to *proposing and designing* ad experiences across the bundle.

By 2025, every brand in the Times' bundle (Games, Cooking, The Athletic, Wirecutter, and News) was generating ad revenue, and ads had become a brand-level KPI where they previously hadn't been. NYT's Q4 2025 digital ad revenue grew 24.9% year-over-year, the strongest growth the company had seen in years. Q1 2026 followed at 31.6% growth and $93.3M in digital ad revenue.<Fn n={1} />

## Major Contribution

### 1. Monetizing the bundle

NYT's [2022 corporate strategy](https://www.nytco.com/press/our-strategy/) reframed the company as essential to daily life beyond news, expanding into games, cooking, sports, product reviews, and audio. Ad strategy followed product strategy: as reader attention spread across the bundle, ad supply needed to follow.

My first major contribution was introducing advertising to [The Athletic](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/01/06/business/new-york-times-the-athletic.html), the sports platform NYT had recently acquired, which had never carried ads. After six months of design work in close collaboration with The Athletic's own design team, [we launched the first ad experience](https://www.nytco.com/press/the-athletic-introduces-advertising/) on the platform in September 2022. The launch landed without breaking subscriber trust or user engagement.

Next was Games. NYT had [bought Wordle](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/01/31/business/media/new-york-times-wordle.html) in early 2022, a massively popular puzzle that had never carried ads. In collaboration with the Games team, we thoughtfully [introduced the first interstitial ad on Wordle](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/new-york-times-wordle-new-mobile-ad-format-doordash/685073/) in July 2023, a format designed to respect the play loop. It proved effective and scaled across the rest of NYT Games.

Cooking was a different kind of expansion — not first-ads, but inventory expansion. We added ad slots to high-traffic recipe and category pages, raising reach and impression volume to a level that opened the door to partnerships like [Cooking × Instacart's shoppable recipes integration](https://www.nytco.com/press/new-york-times-cooking-and-instacart-launch-innovative-shoppable-recipes-integration/). That integration itself wasn't mine to design, but the inventory work that came before is what made a partnership at that scale viable.

Wirecutter was about balance. The product relies on affiliate revenue, so display ads couldn't undercut that flow, and the audience comes for buying advice rather than to be marketed at. I led the work to introduce the first display ads on Wirecutter while protecting the affiliate model, and we built a custom native format, the [Wirecutter Shopper Unit](https://advertising.nytimes.com/formats/display-formats/wirecutter-shopper-unit/), purpose-built for a shopping context.

### 2. Building native ad products

NYT's ad revenue comes from two sources: programmatic placements and native ad experiences. For programmatic, I designed with the user experience in mind, considering where slots appear, when, and what they sit next to. For native, I worked with the ad sales and marketing team to build NYT-owned formats that are unique, premium, and performant. The formats below ship in NYT's public format catalog.

[**Wordle Flex XL**](https://advertising.nytimes.com/formats/display-formats/wordle-flex-xl/). A Games unit where advertisers design their own Wordle. The advertiser brings the brand creative and the words that fill the puzzle; NYT contributes the Wordle game mechanic and visual system. The design balance was protecting Wordle as IP while letting the advertiser's message land clearly, with a system flexible enough to work across any brand.

### 3. Video monetization

Most of the video ads on a Times surface come through programmatic. As the NYT newsroom is investing more in video and visual reporting, the ads playing alongside that journalism have to match its quality. The work was about building a base video ad format that could flex across the bundle without losing editorial quality.

<Figure
  wide
  src="/media/advertising/video-base-spec.png"
  alt="The base video ad format drawn out as a spec — the underlying skeleton with feature toggles called out: skip timing, CTA fade-in, autoplay rules, sound-on and sound-off defaults, length caps, viewability targets"
  caption="The base, drawn out as a spec — feature toggles that turn on or off per surface."
/>

The format is a base spec with features that toggle per surface and placement. Branded features (5s skip, advertiser logo, CTA) can be on or off; some surfaces use the full set, others go barebones. The media space flexes too: full-screen video, inline, different aspect ratios.

### 4. Internal ad tooling

Kaleidoscope is NYT's internal ad data and measurement tool. It supports the full campaign lifecycle: pre (campaign managers pull data to help sales make format recommendations), mid (the system sets targeting and tracks delivery), and post (analysts pull post-buy reports). The users are campaign managers, ad sales, and analytics, the people who keep the ad business running day to day. Whenever I had capacity, I worked on Kaleidoscope, picking up design requests as they came up.

Starting in 2024, more of the work began incorporating AI features — moving in step with the company's broader AI strategy and the maturity of the underlying tools. By late 2025, that included:

- **Smarter search across audience segments**, matching by meaning rather than exact-match keyword, with the ability to ingest long copy or full briefs and return relevant segments.

## Reflection

Advertising comes with constraints most product work doesn't: revenue targets, business commitments, advertiser expectations, partner-team trust. None of that has to dissolve craft. The through-line was design moves that kept ads reader-first inside the constraints: placements that arrive after a reader has engaged with content, formats native to the surface, interaction patterns that set clear expectations. The impact isn't a single launch. It's slow, and it compounds.

Ads borrow space on other teams' products, so every brief involves a partner team whose primary job isn't advertising and whose first answer is often *no*. The skill is staying open instead of defensive: look for mutual goals, come back with more options, trust there's always a way. The work deepened my instincts for resilience, patience, and finding shared ground rather than pushing harder.

Ad work taught me to design for many: not just many products and surfaces (Games, Cooking, Wirecutter, The Athletic, News, podcast, video) but many advertisers with very different assets, messages, brand systems, and goals. A native format spec has to hold up across a milk brand, a streaming service, and a financial advisor, with creative written in different voices and shot at different fidelities. That breadth is its own discipline, and it's reshaped how I think about systems work outside of advertising.
