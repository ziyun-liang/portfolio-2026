## Context

**The role.** When I joined Ad Mission in September 2021 as the team's first product designer, ad design didn't exist as a practice at NYT. Ad Mission was a 60-person organization — mostly product managers and engineers — and ad work itself was the thing other product teams least wanted to take on. Ads were treated as a default disruption: the cost of running the business, tolerated by readers, designed around reluctantly. I reported to a design director who managed me organizationally but wasn't part of Ad Mission's day-to-day; on the ground, I was the design. The reason the role existed: leadership wanted ad design to have its own seat — someone whose first question was *what's best for the reader experience even when an ad is on screen*, and who could push the work toward premium, complementary advertising rather than the disruptive default. The starting brief was News; the work expanded as more brands needed to monetize.

<Margin kind="credit">
  - **Ad Mission product, strategy, eng** — partnership across 60+ people
  - **Brand product teams** — Games, Cooking, Wirecutter, The Athletic, News
</Margin>

**What changed over four years.** The team's design practice scaled with the work. We hired a second product designer; a dedicated design manager joined to run day-to-day. I stayed the most senior design IC throughout. Ad Mission shifted from implementing ad ideas handed to it by other teams, to *proposing and designing* ad experiences across the bundle. By 2025, every brand in the Times' bundle — Games, Cooking, The Athletic, Wirecutter, and News — was generating ad revenue, and ads had become a brand-level KPI where they previously hadn't been.

NYT's Q4 2025 digital ad revenue grew 24.9% year-over-year, the strongest growth the company had seen in years; Q1 2026 followed at 31.6%.

<Margin kind="footnote" n={1}>
  AdWeek, *NYT digital ad growth, Q1 2026* — 24.9% Q4 2025; 31.6% / $93.3M Q1 2026.
</Margin>

Joy Robins, NYT's chief advertising officer, attributed the growth to three things — a clear internal strategy, the lifestyle brand portfolio, and *"a deliberate buildout of ad supply across surfaces that previously had none."* That buildout — surface by surface, brand by brand, format by format — is what this case study is about.

## Major Contribution

### Monetizing the bundle

**The strategic frame.** NYT's [2022 corporate strategy](https://www.nytco.com/press/our-strategy/) reframed the company as essential to daily life beyond news — covering games, cooking, sports, product reviews, audio. Ad strategy followed product strategy: if readers were spending more of their attention across the bundle, ad supply needed to follow. My work over four years was the design side of that buildout — bringing ads to surfaces that hadn't carried them, brand by brand, in a way each brand's audience could accept.

My first major contribution was [The Athletic](https://www.nytco.com/press/the-athletic-introduces-advertising/). About a year after I joined, and after six months of design work in close collaboration with The Athletic's own design team, we introduced advertising to the product (September 2022). The Athletic's subscribers came for sports journalism and hadn't seen ads on the platform — the launch had to land without breaking that trust.

Cooking was a different kind of expansion — not first-ads, but inventory expansion. We added ad slots to high-traffic recipe and category pages, raising reach and impression volume to a level that opened the door to partnerships like [Cooking × Instacart's shoppable recipes integration](https://www.nytco.com/press/new-york-times-cooking-and-instacart-launch-innovative-shoppable-recipes-integration/). ### Building native ad products

**Programmatic carries volume; native carries identity.** Both halves of the ad mix earn the design. We rely heavily on programmatic, and where its slots appear, when they appear, and what they sit next to is its own design decision — a slot placed after the first paragraph, once a reader has gotten something out of the content, reads differently than the same slot dropped at the top. Native formats sit alongside that work: bespoke, NYT-shaped, built for surfaces where a generic IAB unit would feel wrong.

These are the native formats I designed that ship in the public catalog.

[**Wordle Flex XL**](https://advertising.nytimes.com/formats/display-formats/wordle-flex-xl/). The productized form of the Wordle interstitial — first introduced in July 2023, refined over multiple iterations: 5s skip timing, fade-in CTA, 2s skip-button delay, viewability tuning. The principle was respect for the play loop. The ad earns its impression in the natural pause between rounds, rather than by interrupting one.

### Video monetization

**Editorial video and ad video deserve the same craft.** Most of the video ads on a Times surface come through programmatic — but on a product where editorial video is treated as a piece of journalism, the ads playing alongside it have to hold their own. The work in this thread was about building a base video ad format that could flex across the bundle without losing editorial quality at any stop.

<Figure
  wide
  src="/media/advertising/video-base-spec.png"
  alt="The base video ad format drawn out as a spec — the underlying skeleton with feature toggles called out: skip timing, CTA fade-in, autoplay rules, sound-on and sound-off defaults, length caps, viewability targets"
  caption="The base, drawn out as a spec — feature toggles that turn on or off per surface."
/>

**The base, and the adaptations.** Each surface has its own rhythm, and the format had to meet each one. On Games, the ad lives in the pause between rounds, with 5s skip, fade-in CTA, and viewability tuning that respect the play loop. On News' Watch Tab, the format goes full-screen — skip behavior and autoplay tuned for sustained, lean-back viewing rather than interruption. On Cooking, pre-roll and vertical video borrow editorial framing from the recipes themselves. On Connections, podcast (where I led the Podcast Video UX), and in-stream long-form, each surface gets its own autoplay rules, length caps, sound-on or sound-off defaults, and viewability targets.

<Video
  wide
  src="/media/advertising/video-2x2-surfaces.mp4"
  alt="A 2×2 grid showing the same base video ad format adapted to four NYT surfaces — Games' pause-between-rounds, News' Watch Tab full-screen rhythm, Cooking pre-roll with editorial framing, and a podcast app with sound-on defaults"
  caption="Same base, four surfaces — Games, Watch Tab, Cooking, podcast."
/>

The design that pulled it all together: a single spec where features could be turned on or off based on the surface and the product's needs. One format underneath, surface-appropriate behavior on top — so an advertiser could ship a single video creative and have it land sensibly on Wordle, Watch Tab, Cooking, or a podcast app, without any of those surfaces feeling out of character.

### Internal ad tooling

**Kaleidoscope is the system the ad org runs on.** It supports the full lifecycle of a campaign — pre, mid, and post:

- **Pre** — campaign managers pull data and analytics here to help ad sales make format and strategy recommendations.
- **Mid (campaign in flight)** — the system sets targeting segments and tracks delivery.
- **Post** — analysts pull post-buy reports for advertisers.

The users are campaign managers, ad sales, and analytics — the people who keep the ad business running day to day. I've been on and off Kaleidoscope across the four years, picking up redesign threads as they came up.

<Figure
  wide
  src="/media/advertising/kaleidoscope-old.png"
  alt="Kaleidoscope before the redesign — the original dashboard layout, generic data-viewer aesthetic, the starting point for the tooling-based redesign that came later"

Starting in 2024, more of the work began incorporating AI features — moving in step with the company's broader AI strategy and the maturity of the underlying tools. By late 2025, that included:

- **Semantic search across audience segments** — faster finding the segments an advertiser cares about, by meaning rather than exact-match keyword.
- **Brief-page ingestion** — feeding an advertiser's brief into the system and getting a starting set of relevant segments back, before a campaign manager has to assemble them by hand.
- **Ad-format performance and audience-insight redesigns** — surfacing patterns that previously required pulling and crossing multiple reports.
- **A redesigned post-buy report**, including an AI-assistant button and a new eval process — turning what had been a manual reporting exercise into a guided, AI-assisted analysis.

## Reflection

**Reader-focus, even here.** Advertising comes with more constraints than most product work — revenue targets, business commitments, advertiser expectations, partner-team trust to maintain. None of that has to dissolve craft. The through-line across the work was finding the design moves that made ads feel reader-first inside those constraints: thoughtful placements (slots that show up after a reader has gotten something out of the content), formats that read as native to the surface, interaction patterns that are easy to dismiss and set clear expectations. The impact of that work isn't a single launch — it's slow, and it compounds.

**Working in someone else's space.** Ads borrow space on other teams' products. That fundamentally changes how the design conversation works: every brief involves a partner team whose primary job isn't advertising, and whose first answer is often *no*. The skill the work built in me is staying open and collaborative — when a partner team pushes back, I learned not to get defensive. I learned to look for mutual goals, come back with more options, and trust that there's always a way. The work has only deepened those instincts — resilience, patience, and the move to look for shared ground rather than push harder.

**Designing for scale.** Ad work taught me to design for many. Not just many products and surfaces — Games, Cooking, Wirecutter, The Athletic, News, podcast, video — but many advertisers, with very different assets, messages, brand systems, and goals. A native format spec has to hold up across a milk brand and a streaming service and a financial advisor, with ads written in different voices and shot at different fidelities. Designing for that breadth is its own discipline, and it's reshaped how I think about systems work outside of advertising too.
